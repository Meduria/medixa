Reading an ECG Under Pressure

A field guide to the rhythms you're most likely to be quizzed on during clinical rounds.

Cardiology • Clinical rotations level • 7 min read

The five questions to ask, every time

An attending pulling a strip off the monitor and pointing at you is not the moment to start guessing. The rhythms below almost all fall out of the same short checklist, worked through in order:

  1. Rate — roughly how many QRS complexes per minute?
  2. Regularity — is the R-to-R interval constant, or irregular?
  3. P waves — present, absent, or replaced by something else (flutter waves, fibrillatory waves)?
  4. PR interval — normal, prolonged, lengthening, or unrelated to the QRS entirely?
  5. QRS width — narrow (<0.12 s, supraventricular in origin) or wide (≥0.12 s, ventricular or conducted with aberrancy)?

Almost every rhythm on this page is really just a different combination of answers to those five questions. Learn the checklist, and the "quiz me" moment turns into pattern-matching instead of panic.

Normal rate60–100 bpm
Normal PR interval120–200 ms
Normal QRS width<120 ms

Sinus rhythms

These originate from the sinoatrial (SA) node, so every beat has a normal-looking P wave in front of it, and the PR interval is fixed.

Sinus tachycardia >100 bpm

Normal sinus rhythm, just fast. Regular rhythm, normal P wave before every QRS, normal PR interval. Usually a response to something else — fever, pain, hypovolemia, anxiety, hyperthyroidism — rather than a primary cardiac problem.[3]

Sinus bradycardia <60 bpm

Same shape as normal sinus rhythm, just slow. Can be entirely normal in well-conditioned athletes and during sleep, or a sign of high vagal tone, medication effect (beta-blockers), or sinus node disease when it's symptomatic.[3]

Atrial rhythms you'll be quizzed on

Here the electrical trigger comes from somewhere in the atria other than the SA node, or chaotically throughout the atria — so the P wave either disappears, distorts, or multiplies.

Atrial fibrillation irregularly irregular

No organized P waves — just a wavering baseline of fibrillatory activity — and an irregularly irregular R-R interval with a narrow QRS. It's the most common sustained arrhythmia encountered on the wards.[1]

Atrial flutter "sawtooth" waves

A single re-entrant circuit in the atria fires very fast and regularly, producing a classic sawtooth baseline. The AV node protects the ventricle by blocking most of those impulses, commonly conducting every second or third one (2:1 or 3:1).[1]

Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) 150–250 bpm

A very fast, regular, narrow-QRS rhythm, usually with P waves buried in or absent from the preceding T wave. Often starts and stops abruptly and responds to vagal maneuvers or adenosine.[3]

AV blocks: from delay to disconnect

These are graded by how much the electrical conversation between atria and ventricles has broken down — a useful way to think about the four patterns tested on rounds.[1]

1st degreeFixed, prolonged PR (>200 ms); every beat conducts
2nd degree, Mobitz IPR lengthens progressively, then a beat drops (Wenckebach)
2nd degree, Mobitz IIPR is constant, then a beat drops without warning
3rd degree (complete)P waves and QRS complexes march at their own, unrelated rates

The distinction that actually changes management is Mobitz I versus Mobitz II. Mobitz I is usually benign, often from high vagal tone, and rarely needs treatment. Mobitz II reflects structural damage lower in the conduction system, tends to progress unpredictably to complete heart block, and generally warrants pacing.[1]

The rhythms that can't wait

A short list of patterns where recognition has to be immediate, because the next step is a resuscitation algorithm, not a differential diagnosis.[2]

Act first, describe later

  • Ventricular tachycardia (VT): wide-QRS, regular, fast — treat as ventricular in origin until proven otherwise.
  • Ventricular fibrillation (VF): chaotic, disorganized waveform with no identifiable QRS complexes — this is cardiac arrest.
  • Third-degree (complete) heart block: especially with a slow, wide escape rhythm — hemodynamically unstable patients need pacing, not observation.
  • Asystole: a flat line — confirm in more than one lead before calling it, then start CPR.
Knowing how to read and interpret ECGs is a critically important skill in ACLS and PALS. — ACLS Medical Training, "ACLS Rhythm Strips Training and Interpretation"

Key takeaways

  • Work every strip through the same five questions: rate, regularity, P waves, PR interval, QRS width.
  • Sinus rhythms keep a normal P wave and fixed PR interval — only the rate changes.
  • Atrial fibrillation is irregularly irregular with no organized P waves; atrial flutter has sawtooth waves.
  • Mobitz I usually watches and waits; Mobitz II usually gets a pacemaker.
  • VT, VF, and unstable complete heart block are recognize-and-act rhythms, not differentials to sit with.
