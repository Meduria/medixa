pH in biochemistry

pH measures how acidic or basic a solution is on a 0–14 scale: low values mean more free H⁺ (acidic), high values mean less free H⁺ (basic or alkaline), and 7 is neutral.

  • The body keeps blood pH in a narrow range, and buffers — like bicarbonate — resist sudden swings in either direction.
  • Enzymes are picky about pH: each one has an optimal range where it folds and functions best.
  • Pepsin, in the stomach, works best around pH 2; trypsin, released into the small intestine, prefers a mildly basic pH around 8 — each enzyme is tuned to the environment it actually works in.

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are the body's quick-access energy source, built from sugar units strung together in different lengths.

  • Monosaccharides — single sugar units such as glucose, fructose, and galactose; small, but they're the direct fuel cells burn.
  • Disaccharides — two monosaccharides joined together, such as sucrose (glucose + fructose) and lactose (glucose + galactose).
  • Polysaccharides — long chains for storage or structure, such as glycogen in animals and starch in plants.

Lipids

Lipids are a varied family of fat-based molecules, and between them they cover almost every job a cell needs done outside of quick energy.

  • Energy storage — fat holds far more energy per gram than carbohydrate, making it the body's long-term reserve.
  • Structural component of membranes — phospholipids form the bilayer that every cell membrane is built from.
  • Insulation and protection — fat cushions organs and helps retain body heat.
  • Hormones and signalling — steroid hormones, like cortisol and estrogen, are built from lipids.
  • Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins — vitamins A, D, E, and K need dietary fat to be absorbed.

Proteins

Proteins are the body's most versatile molecule, built from amino acid chains folded into specific working shapes.

  • Enzymes — speed up reactions that would otherwise be far too slow to sustain life.
  • Transport — carry other molecules, the way haemoglobin carries oxygen through the blood.
  • Structural — build and support tissue, such as collagen in skin and bone.
  • Signalling — act as messengers between cells, including many hormones.
  • Immunity — antibodies are proteins shaped to recognise specific invaders.
  • Movement — proteins like actin and myosin are what let muscle contract.
