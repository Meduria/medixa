1. Homeostasis

Homeostasis means maintaining the internal body environment constant. It is the internal environment that is held steady, not the external one.

Cells depend on this stability. Keeping normal body temperature is important for normal cellular function, whereas continuously changing blood pH, continuously increasing body water or blood glucose rising above normal would disturb it.

Homeostatic functions are regulated by the nervous system, which acts more rapidly than the endocrine system.

Key point: homeostasis = keeping the internal environment constant; the nervous system is the faster regulator.

2. Feedback Mechanisms

Negative feedback

The response stops or reverses the change that triggered it. It is the most common feedback mechanism in the body.

Positive feedback

The response increases the original stimulus. In blood clotting, activated platelets activate more platelets.

Remember: negative feedback opposes the change; positive feedback strengthens it (clotting).

3. The Cell Membrane

The membrane is built from phospholipid molecules together with cholesterol and proteins.

  • Phosphate head: the hydrophilic part of the phospholipid. The fatty acid tails are the other part of the molecule.
  • Cholesterol: its main function is the regulation of membrane fluidity.

Lipid solubility decides how easily a substance crosses: oxygen has high lipid solubility, while sodium ions, potassium ions and other electrolytes do not.

4. Body Water

In an adult male, water makes up about 60% of body weight.

  • Obesity decreases total body water. Young age, male sex and decreased body fat are not factors that decrease it.
  • The tissue with the highest water content is the gray matter of the brain; bone, adipose tissue and the dentin of teeth hold less.
  • Intracellular fluid contains about two-thirds of total body water. The rest is extracellular fluid, made up of interstitial fluid and plasma.

Memory aid: Na⁺ is the main cation outside; phosphates and proteins are the main anions inside.

6. Passive Transport

Passive transport moves substances down the concentration gradient without energy. It does not use ATP and does not always need a carrier protein.

Simple diffusion

Example: oxygen moving across the cell membrane. It needs no carrier and no ATP.

Facilitated diffusion

Uses carrier proteins (for example, glucose transport through a carrier) but no ATP. When all carriers are saturated, the transport rate reaches a maximum; it does not keep rising and does not become active transport.

Permeability coefficient: the ease or rate at which a substance passes through a membrane under specified conditions.

7. Osmosis

Osmosis is the movement of water across a semipermeable membrane, from high water concentration to low water concentration. It does not use ATP and does not occur only through carrier proteins.

Osmotic pressure is the pressure required to prevent osmosis.

8. Active Transport

Active transport requires ATP and a carrier protein and moves substances against their concentration gradient.

Primary active transport

Example: the Na⁺/K⁺ pump. In each cycle it transports 3 Na⁺ out of the cell and 2 K⁺ in.

Secondary active transport

The energy for the uphill movement comes from the downhill movement of another solute, such as Na⁺. An example of symport (both substances moving together) is Na⁺/glucose cotransport.

9. Exocytosis

Exocytosis functions in the secretion of hormones and the release of chemical transmitters.

Do not confuse: uptake of bacteria into the cell, movement of water across a semipermeable membrane (osmosis) and transport of sodium against its gradient are different processes, not exocytosis.
